### Story

The Wagner family’s relationship with Napa Valley began well before the Caymus label. The family traces its local roots to the nineteenth century, and its timeline records the purchase of Rutherford farmland by Carl Wagner in 1906. Those details help put the winery in context. Caymus was a decision made by a family already familiar with farming, and the work of raising grapes preceded the business of putting a family wine on the market.

In 1972, Charlie and Lorna Wagner established Caymus Vineyards with their son Chuck. The family’s account describes a shared decision to make wine under their own name. It is a useful milestone because it separates the long farming history from the establishment of the winery itself. The year on a Caymus bottle, of course, tells a different story. It identifies the vintage, which allows buyers to distinguish one release from another when the same producer makes several wines from Cabernet Sauvignon.

Over time Cabernet became a central part of how drinkers came to recognise Caymus. The family’s tasting materials present its Napa Valley Cabernet Sauvignon and Special Selection as distinct wines. That distinction is worth keeping in mind when shopping. Read the full wine name and appellation, then look at the vintage and other label details. A listing that says California Cabernet Sauvignon should be understood by the designation on the label. Familiarity with the winery’s Napa Valley history does not assign a Napa Valley appellation to every bottle bearing the name.

The family’s work has extended across generations. Its public account describes Chuck working alongside his children Charlie and Jenny and exploring different parts of California. That larger family portfolio helps explain why a producer search can return more than one label. When choosing a bottle specifically for a Caymus comparison, start with the Caymus-labelled wines and treat other family projects as their own expressions. This keeps the comparison clear and gives each label its own identity.

At the table, the most revealing comparison may be a simple one. Pour a little of each wine, taste in the same order and notice how the fruit and tannin change when a savoury dish is added. Compare the impression of the wine on its own with the impression after a bite. That gives practical meaning to the difference between two bottlings and helps you choose based on the meal, the vintage and the character you enjoy.

### Story

The Dalmore distillery began in 1839 when Sir Alexander Matheson established it on the banks of the Cromarty Firth. Alness lies north of Inverness, and the location gives the whisky a specific home within the Highlands. The distillery’s own history introduces Matheson as a merchant with interests beyond whisky. Founding a distillery brought that commercial background together with a place that would remain central to the identity of the spirit.

The royal stag on the bottle belongs to a different part of the story. The producer associates it with the Mackenzie clan and tells a legend in which Colin of Kintail saves King Alexander III from a charging stag. In that account, the king grants the family the right to use the royal stag emblem. The story is part of the brand’s heritage. The founding of the distillery is a later, documented event. Keeping those two timelines separate makes both the symbol and the history easier to understand.

The relationship between the name and the whisky comes into focus with King Alexander III. The expression takes its name from the figure in the stag story. The producer uses it to show how several cask types can contribute to a single bottling. Its six finishes bring together oak previously associated with different wines and spirits. Port and Madeira are two of the wine connections, while bourbon and Cabernet Sauvignon illustrate how different the cask histories can be. The individual tastes still belong to the finished whisky as a whole.

Cigar Malt Reserve illustrates another choice about wood. Its mix includes bourbon, Matusalem sherry and Cabernet Sauvignon casks. The distillery describes the result in terms of sweetness, spice and structure. The cask information gives drinkers a way to compare the expression with the age-stated 12 Years and with King Alexander III. It also helps explain why the collection can have a family resemblance while offering markedly different pours.

For someone discovering The Dalmore, it can be rewarding to follow that relationship one glass at a time. Notice the aroma before taking a sip and then pay attention to how long the flavour stays after swallowing. Try the same procedure with another expression and keep the glasses at similar temperatures. The stag and the Highland origin connect the bottles. The cask programme, age statement where one appears, and stated strength help explain the differences you can explore at home.

### Story

The Martini & Rossi story begins with three partners in Turin. Alessandro Martini, Teofilo Sola and Luigi Rossi founded the business in 1863, when Turin was the capital of the newly formed Kingdom of Italy. The company’s account places its production facility in Pessione, a hamlet near the city. Turin and Pessione therefore refer to two different pieces of the early history: the city where the partners began the business and the nearby place they chose to make its drinks.

The producer identifies Rossi as an herbalist and liquorist. His background helps explain why botanical flavour is central to the brand’s vermouth story. Vermouth starts with wine and gains character from added herbs and other botanicals. A drinker who knows the base wine style can still be surprised by the aroma and sweetness of the final drink. That is one reason labels such as Bianco and Extra Dry are useful. They give shoppers a starting point for anticipating how the vermouth might taste and what it could do in a mixed drink.

The difference comes through in a small side-by-side pour. Extra Dry is intended for a drier profile. Bianco carries a gentler sweetness, and the producer mentions vanilla and aromatic herbs. Taste each chilled and notice the point at which sweetness gives way to the herbal finish. The result can guide a choice between a vermouth-led drink over ice and a recipe where the vermouth sits alongside a stronger spirit. Start with the amount specified in the recipe, then adjust to your own taste if you make it again.

Sparkling wine gives the Martini name a separate role at the table. Prosecco DOC can be served chilled as a glass of sparkling wine. Its bubbles and fruit are useful if you want something to pour with light food, and it can also form part of a recipe with other ingredients. The fact that the producer makes both sparkling wine and vermouth is helpful when shopping, provided the full bottle name stays in view. The category printed on the label tells you how to use the drink.

The company preserves material from its history in its archives, including bottles, labels and documents. Those records trace how the producer presented its drinks across time.
